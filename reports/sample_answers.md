## How is group cognition different from individual cognition?

Group cognition and individual cognition differ fundamentally in their nature, visibility, and what they can accomplish.

The most basic difference is accessibility: individual cognition is "hidden in private mental processes," while "group cognition is necessarily publicly visible" (Stahl, 2026a, p. 10). This visibility exists because "any ideas involved in a group interaction must be displayed in order for the members of the group to participate in the collaborative process" (Stahl, 2026a, p. 10). This makes group cognition paradoxically easier to study empirically than individual cognition.

At the level of cognitive properties themselves, groups performing cognitive tasks may possess properties differing from those of any individual member (Stahl, 2026b, p. 23). Group cognition involves distinctive processes not reducible to individual thinking. Processes like sequential proposal flow, questioning, building common ground, and collaborative problem-solving "are not analyzable as individual behaviors" (Stahl, 2026b, p. 37).

Groups can achieve what individuals cannot alone. Groups can construct knowledge that no one individual could have constructed alone through a synergistic effect merging ideas from different perspectives (Stahl, 2026c, p. 352). The group knowledge-construction process synthesizes resources from language, culture, the group's history, individual backgrounds, and sequential unfolding of group discourse (Stahl, 2026b, p. 37).

Importantly, while group cognition depends on individual contributions, the group process itself is the proper unit of analysis for understanding how cognition occurs in collaborative contexts (Stahl, 2026b, p. 37). Rather than viewing group cognition as merely the sum of individual minds, a group-level analysis reveals cognitive phenomena that only emerge through interaction at the group unit of analysis (Stahl, 2026b, p. 37).

## What did the Virtual Math Teams project find about how groups do mathematics together?

The Virtual Math Teams project found that groups accomplish mathematics through interactive processes that generate cognition exceeding individual contributions. (Stahl, 2026a, pp. 431–498) Specifically, students collectively develop mathematical conjectures, test them through collaborative discourse, and arrive at group-level conclusions that no individual participant had formulated before the interaction began. (Stahl, 2026a, pp. 431–498)

The project's empirical analyses revealed how group-level mathematical reasoning and argumentation emerge through interaction. (Stahl, 2026a, pp. 431–498) In the dual-space environment combining chat and whiteboard, students progressively build shared understanding by coordinating visual, narrative, and symbolic representations across media. A key methodological finding is that group discourse itself is the primary site where collaborative knowledge building becomes visible. (Stahl, 2026a, pp. 431–498) The project demonstrated that geometry tasks could be accomplished by small groups rather than individuals, with results then shared in larger classroom contexts. (Stahl, 2026b, pp. 21–22) This shows how group mathematical work integrates with broader educational contexts.

## How can teachers be supported in orchestrating computer-supported collaborative learning?

Teachers can be supported in orchestrating CSCL through multiple complementary approaches:

**Integration with curriculum tools:** Most CSCL environments focus on students while neglecting teachers (Stahl et al., 2026, pp. 244–257). To create meaningful educational change, student collaboration tools must be integrated with curriculum development resources that help teachers locate, use, adapt, and share educational materials (Stahl et al., 2026, p. 244). This curriculum support enables teachers to effectively embed collaborative activities into their teaching practice.

**Blended learning design:** Teachers should orchestrate learning by incorporating individual, small-group, and classroom activities in coordinated, mutually supportive sequences (Stahl, 2026a, pp. 88, 96). This blended approach allows teachers to combine student readings and presentations with online peer interaction, followed by classroom discussion and reporting (Stahl, 2026a, p. 96). Teachers play a critical role by introducing new ideas and reviewing student work in the classroom context (Stahl, 2026a, p. 88; Stahl, 2026b, pp. 108–109).

**Real-time dashboards and monitoring tools:** Recent research shows that real-time data visualization supports orchestration by enabling teachers to monitor student progress and provide timely, targeted guidance (Matuk et al., 2019, pp. 251–260). These orchestration technologies should partner with teachers rather than replace their functions, balancing automated support with teacher autonomy (Matuk et al., 2019, pp. 251–260). Specific dashboard features like mirroring support and guiding alerts can help teachers evaluate situations, form goals, and take actions more effectively (Amarasinghe et al., 2021, pp. 307–338).

**Smart assistance systems:** Intelligent technologies can reduce orchestration workload while enabling better interventions. Systems using machine learning to identify critical moments and alert teachers to key intervention opportunities have enabled teachers to successfully guide multiple student groups simultaneously through complex tasks (Tissenbaum & Slotta, 2019, pp. 325–351; Schwarz et al., 2018, pp. 189–211). These tools combine real-time visibility across groups with agent-based support for managing student grouping and resource allocation (Tissenbaum & Slotta, 2019, pp. 325–351).

## What are collaboration scripts and how do they support learner regulation?

Collaboration scripts are instructional structures designed to shape how learners interact with one another during collaborative learning (Stahl et al., 2026, p. 68). They work by specifying a sequence of learning activities, assigning appropriate roles and responsibilities to learners, and coordinating their activities with implicit instructions (Cress et al., 2026, p. 293; Stahl et al., 2026, p. 68). In this way, scripts structure both the social situation and the learning process itself (Cress et al., 2026, p. 293).

Scripts support learner regulation through several mechanisms. First, they provide external guidance and structure in situations where self-regulation of the learning process needs increased support (Cress et al., 2026, p. 293). They function similarly to training wheels on bicycles, allowing students to engage in productive collaboration at higher levels than their own collaborative skills would naturally permit (Stahl, 2026a, p. 152). Scripts also aim to trigger engagement in social and cognitive activities that would otherwise occur rarely or not at all (Stahl et al., 2026, p. 68).

Recent research shows that adaptable scripts—which allow learners to modify components based on their perceived needs—significantly enhance metacognitive reflection and monitoring compared to fixed scripts, and substantially improve learners' self-regulation skills in subsequent unscripted tasks (Wang et al., 2017, pp. 153–172). Additionally, when scripts include regulation macro components that support socio-cognitive and socio-emotional monitoring, the support initiated during scripted phases transfers to subsequent unscripted work (Näykki et al., 2017, pp. 251–279).

However, scripting involves important trade-offs. Over-scripting is detrimental to collaboration, and unnecessary support is demotivating (Stahl, 2026a, pp. 152–153). Effective scripts often confine structured guidance to individual or whole-class activities that precede and follow core small-group interactions, leaving the meaning-making phase itself unconstrained because it is too fragile and complex to be scripted in advance (Stahl, 2026b, p. 381). This approach recognizes that collaborative learning is achieved under unique circumstances whose significance is interactively constructed by learners and cannot be fully predicted (Stahl, 2026b, pp. 378–379).

## Can AI agents or chatbots take part in collaborative learning?

The sources show a strong research direction toward designing AI agents to participate in collaborative learning, though they frame this as an aspiration supported by promising pilot results rather than a settled finding.

Researchers have developed conversational agents specifically intended to participate in student collaborative discussions. These agents are designed to intervene strategically in group conversations: "dialogue agents will seek to direct the interaction between students, offering instruction only as a last resort" (Stahl, 2026a, pp. 149–150). The goal is for agents "to follow the student discourse and to just intervene when needed" (Stahl, 2026b, p. 164), using "automatic analysis of the collaborative-learning process" to detect appropriate moments for intervention (Cui et al., 2026, p. 385).

Pilot evaluations show early promise. Prototype agents have shown positive learning effects in lab and classroom studies in science and engineering, and in middle school fraction arithmetic (Stahl, 2026a, p. 144).

However, a documented classroom study reveals significant challenges. When agents were deployed with students, "the student responses to the agent are problematic. After trying to be responsive, the students give up and start to engage in their own discussion" (Stahl, 2026b, pp. 160–161). In contrast, "the later periods of student interaction show considerable back-and-forth responses" among students themselves. The problem identified is that "the agent speaks like an academic textbook, with no sense of contextualization and little apparent attempt at interaction" (Stahl, 2026b, pp. 160–161).

More recent work with generative AI explores human-computer collaboration for higher-order thinking (Borge et al., 2024, pp. 479–532), finding that ChatGPT supports metacognition and collective sensemaking when users actively regulate conversations with structured reflection prompts (Borge et al., 2024, pp. 479–532). An LLM-based chatbot showed promise for scaling dialogic education, though it faced challenges like negative tone and bias (Ben-David Kolikant et al., 2026, pp. 155–167).

Creating agents that genuinely participate rather than disrupt group dynamics remains an open research challenge.
