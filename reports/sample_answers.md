## How is group cognition different from individual cognition?

Group cognition and individual cognition differ fundamentally in their nature, visibility, and mechanisms, according to the sources.

**Public visibility versus private thought:** The most basic distinction is that group cognition is necessarily publicly visible, whereas individual cognition occurs in private mental processes. Ideas involved in group interaction must be displayed for members to participate in the collaborative process, making group cognition directly observable (Stahl, 2026a, p. 10). This observable character makes group cognition paradoxically easier to study empirically than individual learning (Stahl, 2026a, p. 10).

**Distinct cognitive properties:** A group performing a cognitive task may have cognitive properties that differ from those of any individual member (Stahl, 2026b, p. 23). Groups can construct knowledge that no one individual could have constructed alone through a synergistic effect merging ideas from different perspectives (Stahl, 2026c, p. 352). This means group knowledge is not merely the sum of individual contributions.

**Unit of analysis:** Group cognition operates at the group level of analysis, not the individual level. Group processes—such as sequential proposals, questioning, building common ground, establishing intersubjective meanings, and collaborative problem-solving—are not analyzable as individual behaviors (Stahl, 2026b, p. 37). Although group processes depend on individual contributions and understanding, individual cognition becomes situated within the group process (Stahl, 2026b, p. 37).

**Socio-cultural grounding:** Group cognition integrates language, culture, the group's history, individual backgrounds, relevant contexts, and the sequential unfolding of group discourse (Stahl, 2026b, p. 37). Rather than speculating on hidden psychological processes, studying group cognition focuses on empirically observable group processes of interaction and discourse (Stahl, 2026a, pp. 10–11).

## What did the Virtual Math Teams project find about how groups do mathematics together?

The VMT Project's central finding concerns how group-level mathematical reasoning emerges through collaborative interaction. (Stahl, 2026b, pp. 431–498) shows that students collectively develop mathematical conjectures, test them through collaborative discourse, and arrive at group-level conclusions that no individual participant had formulated before the interaction began. The project demonstrated that "the sequenced construction of shared mathematical meaning" visible in group discourse meets criteria for genuine thinking. (Stahl, 2026b, pp. 431–498)

More specifically, (Stahl, 2026b, p. 463) indicates the project was organized to identify and analyze common practices of online collaborative mathematical problem solving, asking what interactional methods group members engage in to do math and how these methods are proposed, negotiated, and shared. (Stahl, 2026c, pp. 21–22) provides a concrete example of this process: individuals on a team each explore a problem by dragging points; the group makes sense of what is observed; and they are then able to explore dependencies through construction and relate their group understanding to canonical definitions.

The project's theoretical contribution was distinguishing group cognition from related concepts. (Stahl, 2026b, pp. 431–498) argues that group cognition specifically concerns the emergent meaning-making of small groups engaged in computer-mediated interaction, and that the group discourse itself is where the phenomena of collaborative knowledge building are visible. (Stahl, 2026a, p. 130) further suggests that meanings, artifacts, actions and knowledge can be created as the group cognition of online small groups, with the collaboration environment's interaction enabling the group to evolve mathematical practices.

Overall, the VMT Project found that groups achieve mathematical understanding through sustained interactive processes that produce shared meanings exceeding what individuals contribute alone.

## How can teachers be supported in orchestrating computer-supported collaborative learning?

Teachers can be supported in orchestrating computer-supported collaborative learning through several complementary approaches:

**Integration with Technology Tools**

Real-time dashboards and visualization systems provide teachers with timely information about student progress during collaborative activities (Matuk et al., 2019, pp. 251–260). These technologies work most effectively when designed to partner with teachers rather than replace their functions, balancing automated support with teacher autonomy (Matuk et al., 2019, pp. 251–260). More specifically, guiding support in dashboards—which provides alerts and targeted recommendations—enables more focused teacher interactions than simple mirroring of student activity (Amarasinghe et al., 2021, pp. 307–338). Software agents can also reduce teacher workload by dynamically managing student grouping and material allocation while sending timely notifications (Tissenbaum & Slotta, 2019, pp. 325–351).

**Blended Learning Orchestration**

Teachers should orchestrate learning by coordinating individual, small-group, and classroom activities in mutually supportive sequences (Stahl, 2026a, p. 96; Stahl, 2026b, pp. 108–109). Effective blended learning incorporates complementary instructional modes, such as teacher presentations and student readings preceding online peer interaction, followed by classroom discussion (Stahl, 2026a, p. 96). This orchestration allows teachers to introduce new ideas and review student work from pods or individual sessions within the broader classroom context (Stahl, 2026b, pp. 108–109).

**Curriculum Support Tools**

Beyond classroom management, teachers need curriculum development resources integrated with student collaboration tools (Stahl et al., 2026, pp. 244–257). Systems like the Teacher's Curriculum Assistant support teachers in locating, using, adapting, and sharing curriculum resources, enabling them to redesign instruction for different modalities and learning contexts (Stahl et al., 2026, pp. 244–257). Without such integration of collaborative activities into curricula, technology efforts will have limited widespread educational impact (Stahl et al., 2026, p. 244).

**Participatory Design**

Effective support systems require participatory design involving educators throughout development (Matuk et al., 2019, pp. 251–260), ensuring that teacher needs and constraints shape technology solutions from the outset.

## What are collaboration scripts and how do they support learner regulation?

Collaboration scripts are instructional tools designed to structure how learners interact with one another (Stahl et al., 2026, p. 68). They work by specifying "a sequence of learning activities, together with appropriate roles for the learners" to trigger engagement in social and cognitive activities that would otherwise occur rarely or not at all (Stahl et al., 2026, p. 68). More specifically, scripts assign roles and responsibilities to learners and coordinate their activities (Cress et al., 2026, p. 293).

A key framework for scripting involves operating at multiple levels: scripts typically organize teacher-centered classroom activities, then transition to the small-group level where peer collaboration occurs, and finally structure individual and class-level activities to consolidate learning (Stahl & Hesse, 2026, p. 77). This multi-level integration is essential because the small-group interaction is key to collaborative learning (Stahl & Hesse, 2026, p. 77).

Regarding learner regulation, scripts support this through several mechanisms. Scaffolding through scripts increases productive collaboration behavior beyond what students could achieve independently (Stahl, 2026, p. 152), functioning like training wheels that allow learners to collaborate at higher levels than their natural skills permit. However, this support must be responsive: research shows that "over-scripting is detrimental to collaboration" and "scaffolding should be faded over time" (Stahl, 2026, pp. 152–153).

Recent evidence demonstrates more nuanced benefits. Adaptable scripts—allowing learners to modify components based on self-perceived needs—significantly increased metacognitive reflection and monitoring compared to fixed scripts, and improved self-regulation skills in subsequent unscripted tasks (Wang et al., 2017, pp. 153–172). Additionally, regulation scripts can facilitate sustained collaborative regulation: socio-emotional support initiated during scripted phases transferred to unscripted work (Näykki et al., 2017, pp. 251–279). Integrating scripts with group awareness and self-regulation mechanisms yields more effective learning environments than addressing these approaches separately (Schnaubert & Vogel, 2022, pp. 1–10).

## Can AI agents or chatbots take part in collaborative learning?

Yes, AI agents and chatbots can participate in collaborative learning, though research shows this requires careful design.

Conversational agents have been deployed in various collaborative learning environments. In virtual math team settings, "agents in VMT would participate in the student conversation" (Cui et al., 2026, p. 385). Prototype agents have shown positive results: they have "yielded positive learning effects in our pilot evaluations in lab and classroom studies" across science, engineering, and middle school mathematics (Stahl, 2026a, p. 144).

However, effective agent participation depends on how they engage. Research suggests agents should "seek to direct the interaction between students, offering instruction only as a last resort" (Stahl, 2026a, pp. 149–150), focusing on eliciting elaborated explanations during help-seeking interactions (Stahl, 2026a, pp. 149–150). A key challenge is that agents must be responsive to students' actual discourse. One study found problematic student responses when the agent "speaks like an academic textbook, with no sense of contextualization and little apparent attempt at interaction," whereas students showed more engagement when interacting with each other (Stahl, 2026b, pp. 160–161).

More recent work explores generative AI tools in collaborative contexts. Research indicates that generative AI can support both individual and collective thinking when users employ structured reflection and actively manage conversations, though human oversight remains important for effective learning (Borge et al., 2024, pp. 479–532). Another study found that "agent suggestions better improved learning gains" when combined with other collaborative supports (Hayashi, 2020, pp. 469–498).

The effectiveness of AI participation thus hinges on designing agents to be contextually responsive and to facilitate rather than dominate student interaction.
