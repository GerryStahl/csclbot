**Turn 1: What are collaboration scripts?**

Collaboration scripts are structured frameworks designed to guide how learners interact with one another during collaborative learning. (Stahl et al., 2026, p. 68) They specify a sequence of learning activities together with appropriate roles for learners, and are designed to trigger engagement in social and cognitive activities that would otherwise occur rarely or not at all. The scripts operate using several key components: participants, activities, roles, resources, and groups, along with mechanisms for task distribution, group formation, and sequencing. (Stahl et al., 2026, p. 68)

More theoretically, collaboration scripts work by providing external support based on the concept of internal collaboration scripts. (Strauß et al., 2025, pp. 123–124) Internal collaboration scripts are flexible cognitive structures consisting of knowledge and expectations about the structure of a situation, including its phases, roles, and the actions necessary to accomplish collaborative goals. (Strauß et al., 2025, pp. 123–124) When learners lack effective internal scripts, external collaboration scripts can scaffold their engagement by providing guidance one level above learners' existing capabilities. (Strauß et al., 2025, pp. 148–149)

A key characteristic of collaboration scripts is that they attempt to address a central tension in CSCL: collaborative learning is achieved under unique circumstances that cannot be predicted, yet computer support attempts to define specific contexts and direct activity. (Stahl, 2026b, pp. 378–379) Rather than rigid implementable plans, scripts are better understood as situated resources that learners interpret and make sense of through their interactions. (Stahl, 2026b, p. 374)

Collaboration scripts can take various forms—from highly structured sequences to relatively flexible approaches, such as guided-reciprocal-questioning scripts that allow students to select questions and formulate their own. (Stahl, 2026a, pp. 163–164) Effective scripts often integrate activities across multiple social levels: individual, small-group, and classroom levels, creating coherent pedagogical processes. (Stahl & Hesse, 2026, p. 77)

**Turn 2: How do they support learner regulation?**

*Searched for: How do collaboration scripts support learner regulation?*

Collaboration scripts support learner regulation through several integrated mechanisms. Most directly, external collaboration scripts can target self-regulation skills by scaffolding learners' strategies for regulating their learning through self-, co-, and shared regulation (Schnaubert & Vogel, 2022, pp. 3–4). This scaffolding of regulation can be built into the script design itself (Schnaubert & Vogel, 2022, pp. 3–4).

A key approach involves structuring activities across multiple levels. Scripts can support regulation of tasks that are not naturally collaborative—such as reading—by positioning solitary reading between opening collaborative planning and concluding collaborative review episodes (Crook, 2022, p. 172). This creates space for learners to regulate their engagement with the core activity.

However, the effectiveness of scripts in supporting regulation depends significantly on learner characteristics. Learners with higher self-regulation skills tend to benefit more from adaptable scripts and minimal scaffolding (Vogel et al., 2022, p. 58). These learners with stronger self-regulation skills "tended to decide more often in favor of the minimal CSCL scripts" and showed higher learning outcomes (Vogel et al., 2022, p. 58). This suggests that adaptable scripts work best for learners with existing self-regulation capacities (Vogel et al., 2022, p. 60).

An alternative approach moves beyond prescriptive scripts toward designs that "transfer the responsibility for regulation to the group and convey knowledge about collaboration without coercing learners during interaction" (Strauß et al., 2025, p. 125). Through repeated guided practice with external scripts, learners develop the components of an effective internal collaboration script (Strauß et al., 2025, p. 124), building their own regulatory capacities over time.

**Turn 3: Are there studies that tested this experimentally?**

*Searched for: Are there experimental studies that tested how collaboration scripts support learner regulation, particularly regarding the relationship between learner self-regulation skills and the effectiveness of different levels of script scaffolding?*

Yes, there are several experimental studies in the sources that tested how collaboration scripts support learner regulation and the relationship between learner self-regulation skills and script effectiveness.

One key experimental study examined adaptable collaboration scripts with 87 university students across three phases. Results showed that adaptable scripts significantly increased metacognitive reflection and monitoring activities compared to fixed scripts, and substantially improved learners' self-regulation skills in subsequent unscripted tasks (Wang et al., 2017, pp. 153–172). Mediation analyses revealed that reflection processes during scripted collaboration explained the positive effects on later regulation skill transfer (Wang et al., 2017, pp. 153–172).

Another experimental study with 150 university students compared external collaboration scripts and collaborative reflection scaffolds for fostering collaboration skills (Strauß et al., 2025, pp. 121–159). Using multilevel modeling, researchers found that learners receiving collaborative reflection gained significantly more knowledge about effective collaboration than those using scripts or no support, though neither intervention improved subsequent interaction quality (Strauß et al., 2025, pp. 121–159).

A third study, reported by Vogel et al., specifically tested how learner self-regulation skills interact with different levels of script scaffolding. More specific analyses showed that learners with higher self-regulation skills "tended to decide more often in favor of the minimal CSCL scripts," and learners who adapted their scaffolding more often to minimal scripts showed higher learning outcomes regarding socio-discursive skills (Vogel et al., 2022, p. 58).

These experimental studies collectively demonstrate that script design choices matter for regulation: learners with stronger self-regulation benefit from adaptable and minimal scaffolding, while the effectiveness of scripts depends on matching scaffolding levels to learner capabilities (Vogel et al., 2022, p. 60).

**Turn 1: How can teachers be supported in orchestrating collaborative learning?**

Teachers can be supported in orchestrating collaborative learning through multiple complementary strategies:

**Integration of Tools and Curriculum Resources**

Teachers need integrated support systems that combine student collaboration tools with curriculum development resources. This allows fundamental collaborative activities—locating, using, adapting, and sharing—to operate at both teacher and student levels, creating coherent classroom support (Stahl et al., 2026, p. 244).

**Orchestrating Blended Learning Sequences**

Rather than relying solely on collaborative sessions, teachers should orchestrate sequences that integrate individual, small-group, and classroom learning in mutually supportive ways (Stahl, 2026a, p. 96). For example, "a teacher presentation and student readings can precede online peer interaction, which is followed up by classroom discussion and reporting" (Stahl, 2026c, p. 116). This approach requires teachers to establish a culture of collaboration over time through redefined procedures and expectations (Stahl, 2026c, p. 116).

**Technology-Enhanced Orchestration Support**

Orchestration technologies can reduce teacher burden by providing real-time support. Dashboards with different support provisions—such as mirroring (showing current group status) and guiding (providing alerts and recommendations)—help teachers manage the complex demands of monitoring multiple groups simultaneously (Amarasinghe et al., 2021, pp. 307–338). Real-time dashboards enable teachers to monitor student progress and provide timely, targeted guidance during complex collaborative activities (Matuk et al., 2019, pp. 251–260).

**Developing Teacher Expertise**

However, technology alone is insufficient. Teachers need adaptive expertise to know when and how to intervene without disrupting students' disciplinary thinking (Pietarinen et al., 2021, p. 262). Teacher practices—including skillful classroom orchestration and making strategic rounds—significantly impact collaborative learning outcomes (Stahl, 2026b, pp. 178–179). Teacher support and affect also foster productive learning environments (Pietarinen et al., 2021, p. 262).

**Turn 1: What is intersubjectivity?**

Intersubjectivity is the ability of people to understand each other (Stahl, 2026a, pp. 48–49). More specifically, it indicates shared understanding among people, but this "sharing" is not a matter of individuals holding similar understandings (Stahl, 2026b, p. 302; Stahl, 2026e, pp. 61–62). Rather, it involves people participating productively in joint meaning-making discourse within a communal world (Stahl, 2026b, p. 302; Stahl, 2026e, pp. 61–62).

A group achieves intersubjectivity when its members interact well enough to pursue the group's aims (Stahl, 2026b, p. 302). This shared understanding must be built up gradually through interaction and repaired frequently (Stahl, 2026b, p. 302). The concept points to a fundamental question: whether knowing, thinking, or being aware are at base matters of individual consciousnesses or of collectivities (Stahl, 2026d, p. 208; Stahl, 2026c, p. 285).

Philosophically, intersubjectivity can refer to two related but distinct ideas (Stahl, 2026c, pp. 285–286). It can mean the problem of how two or more minds can inter-relate—understand each other and work together from their individual cognitive positions. It can also refer to a form of joint cognition that is shared by a group and transcends, unifies, or even founds the cognition of the participating individuals (Stahl, 2026c, pp. 285–286).

In the context of collaborative learning, intersubjectivity is considered the defining characteristic of CSCL (Stahl, 2026b, p. 302; Stahl, 2026e, pp. 61–62). It is what makes collaboration and collaborative learning possible (Stahl, 2026e, pp. 61–62). Recent studies suggest that intersubjectivity structures group cognition, which can provide a foundation for collaboration in work and learning that incorporates but transcends individual cognition (Stahl, 2026d, p. 208; Stahl, 2026c, p. 285). The concept thus shifts focus from individual minds to the shared understanding and joint activities of groups.
